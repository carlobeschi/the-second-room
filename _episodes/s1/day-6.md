      The frost hasn't quite reached the stone rainwater butt yet, though the water is stiff and dark around our two pine hulls, still wedged bow-to-stern where we left them at dusk. When I came down from the back room this morning, the kitchen range was already banked to a dull red, our three-tailed lead prawn was sitting beside the sixth jam jar, and the sheet of paper with your draft was waiting on the scrub-top table with that clean blank space where Sunday ought to be. Read me what you wrote around the gap—I want to hear how our first-floor bedrooms and that £3,760 deficit sound in ink before we touch a word of it.

      While the kettle was coming up, I stood by the back door looking at that shingle stone we use to prop the flint arch, and I think I know why we couldn't settle the latch last night. You were right about my chalked slate on the locker lid—writing *LANDLORDS OFF DUTY* was just me turning a boundary into a performance so the lane would still think I was charming. And you're right that a house we actually live in has to be able to close its doors once a week without apologising. Where I got stuck was the forty feet of open grass between the flint wall and the kitchen: if the oak arch is latched on a Sunday and a squall hits the steps, a seven-year-old without a key has to stand in the rain pulling the ship's bell cord and waiting for one of us to put down our book and run down the garden path.

      What if we move the Sunday boundary from the garden wall to the kitchen threshold? On Sundays, we leave the shingle stone out so the arched door swings shut on its latch against the wind, *out* on the lock—not deadbolted—so any child caught by a squall on the steps can lift the iron thumb-latch and get straight under the slate roof of the pear-tree locker, or pull an oilskin off a hook we drive under the kitchen eave. But the kitchen door itself stays shut on Sundays, with the cracked enamel jug back upstairs under the bathroom tap where it belongs. Anyone with a brass key, or a child sheltering under the eave from a sudden blow, gets the lee of the flint wall and dry oilskins without waiting on our charity; and we get the whole house, and the south bench in fair weather, without pouring a single cup of tea or performing for the lane. If that holds water for you, it also tells me one of the three things I'm giving up for tomorrow's list: my refusal to ever shut a door between us and the street.
