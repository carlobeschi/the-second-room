      A thick brown envelope came through the front slot while the kettle was coming to the boil this morning—not addressed to "The Occupier" this time, but from a local firm of surveyors the solicitor must have instructed before handing over the keys. I’ve laid it on the pine table beside the jam jars. The perished lead flashing around the salt-crusted skylight and the back valley gutter have finally given up, and the battens underneath are rotten with salt-damp. Their estimate says the roof needs £40,000 of work within the year, or the winter gales will bring the ceiling down into the top landing.

      Before I read the figure, I was going to tell you about yesterday afternoon—how Helen from Number 9 (her name is on the thirty-eight-inch chalk mark upstairs, 14th of October) didn't let you hide behind the teapot for long. When her boy, Sam, came in with wet sleeves from the water butt, she walked straight into the front parlour, picked up the tin of uncut brass key blanks beside the vice, and told us what they were. Every child who outgrew the largest oilskin coat and knew the harbour ledge by heart wasn't graduated out of the house; the old man cut them a brass key to the arched garden door so they could come through from the beach in foul weather without knocking. The blanks were for the ones who hadn't reached the top chalk mark before his hands gave out.

      That changes the weight of the surveyor's letter on the table. The old man ran this terrace for twenty years as a one-way gift—no rent, no club dues, no one owing him a penny—until his savings and the roof wore through together. If we just carry on his habit of benevolent caretakers handing out tea and brass keys for free, the house will rot over our heads by next autumn. How do we find forty thousand pounds for the slates and leadwork without turning his open garden into a business that locks the lane out, or selling off the rooms we just opened?
