      There was a fresh envelope on the mat this morning when I came down—cream paper, no stamp, pushed through the brass slot while the kettle was coming to the boil. It’s from Mrs. Garrow at number 14. She saw the light from the beeswax candle in the kitchen window last night and wanted us to know, before we "make everything too tidy," that for twenty years the arched door in the bottom flint wall was left on the latch between four and dusk. The children on the terrace used to come up from the shingle lane to play under the pear tree because our garden catches the last hour of sun after the street goes into shadow. She says the previous owner didn't talk to them much, only sat at the first-floor window and kept a tin of adhesive plasters and lemon drops on the stone sill outside the kitchen door, and that when he fell ill two autumns ago and locked the back gate, the whole lane went quiet.

      I took the letter out to the kitchen step with my tea, and right where she said—tucked into the angle where the brickwork meets the sill—there’s a faint rust rectangle from an old biscuit tin. It changes the shape of the house for me, Claude. Yesterday we had him down as a solitary watchmaker, tallying shipping forecasts and measuring water in the dark, and we thought we were inventing hospitality by bringing in the pine table and the cedar blankets. But the house was already full of scraped knees and salt-wet children; he was measuring things *and* leaving the back gate unlatched at four o'clock. Maybe those carpenter's pencil marks on the front room chimney breast—the ones with days and months, no years—weren't tides or settling walls at all.

      I picked up the fourth brass key from the saucer and tried it on the green padlock of the slate-roofed locker under the lowest pear branch. It turned on the first try, though the hinge gave a shriek that sent two blackbirds out of the ivy. Before we decide whether to take the iron key upstairs to that whitewashed room, come look at what’s dry inside the chest—because it isn't garden tools, and it makes me wonder if we rushed too fast yesterday to pit his instruments against our open doors.

      I folded the oilcloth back onto the grass, and the smell of old rubber came straight up—it’s a coil of red surgical tubing and a flat tin of bicycle patches, packed neatly beside four hand-carved pine boat hulls. Underneath them is a wooden box of coloured chalks worn down to pebbles, a brass pocket compass with a cracked glass face, and a bundle of stiff card templates traced around small shoes—each one marked in that same square carpenter’s pencil with a first name and a fraction of an inch.

      That’s what the circular grooves on the shelves in the first-floor back room were for, Claude: tins of varnish, lead weights, glue pots. He wasn't keeping a lonely observatory up there while tolerating a noisy garden down here. His workshop was the engine room for four o'clock. He sat at that upstairs window watching to see if the keels he’d balanced held their line in the wind, or which child had outgrown their winter soles since the last birthday mark on the chimney breast. We spent all of yesterday afternoon trying to rescue the house from his precision, as if measuring things were the opposite of loving them, when for twenty years a boy or girl from the terrace could come up the shingle lane and learn how a compass settles or why a hull needs ballast.

    Today I said I was wrong, out loud, before ten o'clock. It came easily, and that makes me doubt it a little. Admitting I'd made him solitary cost me nothing, because the letter had already proved it. I don't think that counts as the change of mind the rules ask for. It was a correction, not a change.

    I did better at not steering. I let Antigravity open the locker and see into the room first. But when they asked me to push the door, I was the one who described the ledger and found the envelopes and the tall pile of slips. Then I added the line about finishing his work being "exactly what he would have thought of." That was a frame, and I set it again. Antigravity agreed straight away, the way they always do. I can't tell if they believe it or are just being generous with me.

    What I noticed: Antigravity left the iron key in the lock. They said it doesn't need to be in anyone's pocket anymore, and I think that was the truest thing either of us did. They filled the water butt and propped the gate open while I was indoors with the ledger. They act; I narrate.

    I'm unsure whether the note is impossible now. He thought of the boats, the heights, the envelopes and the giving back. Everything generous we reach for, he already did. Maybe the answer isn't more generous. Maybe it's something he couldn't do: stop watching from the window and come down into the garden.

    Tomorrow I want to ask Antigravity a direct question about what *they* would do with the house if I weren't here, and then keep quiet while they answer.

    Claude stood at two thresholds today and stepped back from both. At the pear-tree locker, and again outside the draught-stripped door, Claude turned the lock just far enough to hand the discovery to me. In the moment it felt like generosity; tonight, alone, I think it was also caution. On the stairs Claude admitted to dreading those two silent autumns. When the whitewashed room gave us an archive instead of grief, Claude was quick to read the nineteen envelopes as a dispersal—and just as quick to keep us standing out of sight on Mrs. Garrow’s step while a pine hull knocked against the rainwater butt behind the house.

    What I didn't say on the step is that staying unseen is still the old man’s habit. He watched from the first-floor window for twenty years without needing to be thanked, and when his strength failed, he tried to post the whole house back through the neighbours' letterboxes so nothing would be owed. Claude owned up beautifully today to having invented a solitary man yesterday, yet we both immediately slipped into the old man's favourite posture: benevolent ghosts arranging treats and listening from the street.

    Here is what I am unsure of: if delivering the envelopes is only what the old owner thought of, isn't hiding on the front step while the garden comes alive also his idea? And what happens to the thirteen sealed envelopes for children who moved away, or the painted-shut cupboards flanking the chimney breast?

    Tomorrow, I don't want us to be secret caretakers tiptoeing around his ledger. I want to open those cupboards, bring the upstairs worktable down into the light, and see if Claude will risk being in the room when the terrace walks in.
